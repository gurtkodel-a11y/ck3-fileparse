Playtest 2 — Initial Assessment
=========================================================

Focus for this playtest: narrative tools for character interaction.
This assessment therefore gives extra attention to the character web
around Vratislav — who these people are, what tensions exist between
them, and where the richest narrative material lives.


I. THE OPENING POSITION
-----------------------

Bohemia opens in the same structural shape as Playtest 1 (1066.9.15):
seven personal holdings in peacetime with full control, a familiar MaA
composition (zbrojnosh Heavy Infantry, Light Horsemen, Pikemen, Mangonel),
and the same Czech cultural traditions (Mountaineer Ruralism, Hard Working,
Castle Keepers, Life Is Just A Joke). The realm ceiling — 3,287 maximum
troops — is nearly identical. Praha leads at development 12; the six
remaining holdings sit at 8. Čáslav's special economic site remains the
highest-priority early development investment.

The vassal ledger, likewise, follows the same shape: Konrád Přemyslid
(Count of Brno, 1,391 troops, three Moravian holdings) anchors the east as
the dominant feudal vassal; Ota Přemyslid (Count of Olomouc, 869 troops)
flanks him; Česlav Vrsovic (Count of Žatec) is the only non-Přemyslid
feudal count; and a ring of republic and theocracy barons fills the margins.

One thing has visibly shifted from 1066.9.15 to 1066.10.2: the theocracy
count of Opava is no longer Jaromír, and Jaromír is no longer a vassal at
all. The implications of that shift run through most of this assessment.


II. THE CHARACTER WEB
---------------------

This is where Playtest 2 diverges sharply from Playtest 1 in character,
and where the narrative richest material lies.

--- Jaromír (id 34156): The Rival in Exile ---

In Playtest 1, Jaromír Přemyslid held Opava as a theocracy count —
Vratislav's declared rival sitting inside the realm, impossible to revoke,
generating a continuous opinion drain. That situation was poisonous but
contained.

In Playtest 2, Jaromír is unlanded and at another court. The mutual rival
relationship persists — both sides have declared enmity — but the locus of
danger has shifted. An unlanded rival inside your realm is a political
irritant. An unlanded rival at a foreign court is a different shape of
threat: mobile, potentially sponsored, and accumulating grievances without
the moderating responsibilities of holding land. He has every incentive to
seek a patron, a claim, or a faction; he has no territory to lose if he
gambles.

From a narrative standpoint, Jaromír is the most compelling character in
the opening position. He is almost certainly Přemyslid — a kinsman, perhaps
a brother or cousin, cast out or never given land in this timeline. The
classic exile arc applies: Where is he? Who has taken him in? What does
he want? The answers to those questions will eventually come back to
Bohemia's door.

The practical concern: an unlanded rival at a foreign court is much easier
to invite back, marry off to neutralize, or eventually destroy than an
entrenched theocracy bishop — but he is also capable of suddenly becoming
the figurehead for someone else's ambitions. Keep track of his location.

--- Jan (id 32280): The Friend in the Counting House ---

Jan holds Opava as its theocracy count — the county Jaromír no longer holds.
He contributes zero levy (as expected for a Church vassal) but returns
ecclesiastical income. More unusually: he is Vratislav's declared mutual
friend. A bishop who is both your vassal and your personal friend is a
combination that appears rarely and matters more than the numbers suggest.

The Pope has a −30 impious opinion of Vratislav as of this date. That is
a significant deficit for a Catholic ruler, and it will compound if
Vratislav draws censure from Rome or is caught in the Investiture conflict
on the wrong side. Jan — a friendly theocracy vassal — is a potential
narrative counterweight: a churchman inside Bohemia who can vouch for his
duke, carry messages to the Curia, or quietly absorb some of the friction
between Vratislav and the institutional Church. His friendship is worth
tending precisely because the Pope is not impressed.

Narratively, Jan is the archetype of the loyal bishop-counselor: a learned
man without military ambition, embedded in the court's life, connected to
the Church hierarchy, and personally bound to the duke by friendship rather
than mere obligation. That is a character worth developing.

--- Šwiétosława (id 36471): The Polish Duchess ---

Vratislav's current wife carries a Polish-heritage name (Šwiétosława) and
sits on the council as primary spouse. His former wife Adelaida died in
January 1062; this marriage is likely from the early 1060s or the game
start. She is unlanded and currently in Vratislav's court.

Poland (Bolesław Piast, 2,725 troops, local zone, same heritage) is the
most significant secular neighbor of comparable tier. A Polish-born wife
creates an implicit personal connection to the Piast dynasty that no formal
alliance yet formalizes. Whether that connection is a diplomatic asset
(leverage toward a negotiated coexistence) or a liability (pressure from
Bolesław to favor Polish interests) depends on the eventual trajectory of
Czech-Polish relations.

Narratively, Šwiétosława is the most under-examined character in the opening
court. She is present, educated enough to hold a council role, and culturally
distinct from Bohemia's Czech core. A Polish duchess in a Czech court is
a woman navigating two worlds; her perspective on the court's internal
politics — particularly the Přemyslid family dynamics — would be a rich
first-person narrative thread.

--- The Přemyslid Children (Court) ---

All six of Vratislav's children are in court: Judita, Břetislav, Ludmila,
Boleslav, Bořivoj, and Vladislav. Their parentage divides the court subtly
— some are Adelaida's, some Šwiétosława's — though the save does not
distinguish this directly.

House-head hooks attach to three of the six: Boleslav, Bořivoj, and
Vladislav — but not to Judita, Břetislav, or Ludmila. This asymmetry is
worth examining. Bretislav as an un-hooked son is a potential succession
tension point: if he is the eldest male heir, the absence of a hook means
Vratislav cannot leverage him as readily. The three hooked sons (Boleslav,
Bořivoj, Vladislav) may be younger and their hooks reflect Vratislav's
position as house head rather than deliberate political strategy — but the
question of which son is favored, and why, is inherently narrative.

Judita as the eldest named daughter carries the most immediate marriage
value. She is an untouched diplomatic asset at game start.

--- The Knights: A More Capable Pool ---

Playtest 2 opens with six knights rather than Playtest 1's five, and the
composition is stronger across the board: Záviš (prowess 10), Boreš (9),
Vznata (9), Veliš (9), Ota (6), Cçaslav (7). The middle tier is
noticeably deeper — three prowess-9 knights is unusual at game start.

Knights in CK3 are not just mechanical units; they are named characters
with relationships, ambitions, and dynastic weight of their own. Záviš
and Vznata are also listed as courtiers (not just military retainers),
which means they are embedded in court life, potentially holding council
roles later, and generating the ordinary drama of feudal household
politics. A prowess-10 knight named Záviš who also sits at court is a
character waiting for a story.

--- Konrád and Ota (Moravia): The Family Bloc ---

Konrád Přemyslid (Brno, 1,391 troops) and Ota Přemyslid (Olomouc, 869
troops) are the structural fact of Bohemia's internal politics: the
Moravian Přemyslids hold more combined military weight than Vratislav
himself. They are family — which provides cultural loyalty glue — but
family loyalty in the Přemyslid tradition has historically been a
contingent rather than a guaranteed quantity.

Konrád in particular is the character to watch. He is the most powerful
individual in the realm after Vratislav. His relationship with Jaromír
(the exiled rival) is unrecorded in the data; they may be allies by blood,
or Konrád may be entirely neutral to Jaromír's fate. Either way, if Jaromír
returns and seeks Přemyslid family support, Konrád is the pivot point.

--- The Anonymous Court: OjíŘ, Cčenéek, Zbyslav, Svatobor ---

Four non-family, non-knight courtiers fill the household: OjíŘ (id 63538),
Cčenéek (id 63539), Zbyslav (id 63540), and Svatobor (id 37565). Svatobor
has a noticeably older save-file ID than the others, suggesting he has been
at court — or in the save file's history — longer than the rest.

These are the minor characters who give texture to court life: the secretary,
the steward's assistant, the aging retainer, the ambitious young man. In
Playtest 1, the court had a different composition at this date. In Playtest 2,
these specific four are the fabric of Vratislav's household. Their individual
traits, education, and claims are worth surfacing as the narrative layer
develops — each is a potential scene partner for any advisor-level character
interaction tool.


III. THE EXTERNAL SITUATION
---------------------------

The strategic environment is unchanged from the Playtest 1 opening assessment
and summarized briefly here:

  The HRE (Heinrich III Salian, 5,531 troops, 152,120 power) is Bohemia's
  overlord and the dominant regional frame. Heinrich is on the eve of the
  Saxon War and Investiture conflict — opportunities for choosing sides
  advantageously, obligations to be managed.

  Poland (Bolesław Piast, 2,725 troops, same heritage, local zone) is the
  most significant Catholic secular neighbor and the primary external
  uncertainty. No alliance, no conflict — a relationship in suspension.
  Šwiétosława is the personal bridge.

  Hungary (Salamon Árpád, 2,674 troops, nearby, Catholic) is the natural
  first formal alliance target: same faith, adjacent, no competing interests.

  The pagan belt (Pomerania, Luticia, Veletia, Baltic pagans) sits to the
  north and northwest — combined strength exceeding the whole Bohemian realm,
  but fragmented and not presently mobilized against Bohemia.

  Byzantium (Konstantinos Doukas, 6,551 troops, orthodox) is the strongest
  power in the geographic frame but separated from Bohemia by Hungary and
  several Catholic kingdoms.

  William of Normandy (6,803 troops) is weeks from Hastings. His conquest
  of England reorders western Europe's attention. Neutral for Bohemia.

Bohemia holds zero formal alliances at this date — a significant diplomatic
gap given the vassal-weight ratio internally. The first priority after
stabilizing the character web is securing at least one meaningful external
partner before the HRE's internal crises demand choices.


IV. THE PAPAL PROBLEM
---------------------

Anselmo, as Pope, holds a −30 impious opinion of Vratislav. This is a
named-character relationship worth noting separately because it intersects
the narrative layer directly: piety in CK3 is not just a number but a
reputation with the Church as an institution, and the Pope is both a
mechanical actor (excommunication, crusades) and a character.

The −30 is steep for an opening position. It may reflect a lifestyle trait,
a prior event, or a heresy suspicion — the save does not record the opinion
source directly. Whatever its origin, it means the Pope's goodwill toward
Bohemia is already depleted before the Investiture conflict begins.

Jan of Opava (the friendly bishop-vassal) is the most obvious in-realm
counterweight. Investing in a pilgrimage, a church building program, or
simply keeping Bohemia clearly inside the Latin Christian fold during the
coming schism are the mechanical levers; the narrative expression of those
choices — a duke seeking to repair his reputation with Rome while managing
a rival exile and an underdeveloped domain — is precisely the kind of
first-person tension that character interaction tools should surface.


V. PRIORITIES AND NARRATIVE THREADS FOR PLAYTEST 2
---------------------------------------------------

Strategic priorities (carry forward from Playtest 1 logic):
  1. Locate Jaromír — where is he, who is hosting him, can he be neutralized,
     married off, brought home on terms, or safely ignored?
  2. Repair the Papal opinion — the −30 impious deficit is a liability as the
     Investiture conflict approaches; early investment in piety costs little.
  3. Secure a formal alliance — Hungary is the clearest first move; Poland
     may be reachable via the Šwiétosława connection.
  4. Develop Čáslav — the special economic site returns above-average yields
     on development investment; prioritize it in the building queue.
  5. Monitor Konrád of Brno — not hostile, but structurally dominant in
     Moravia; favorable marriages into his family are worth arranging early.

Narrative threads for character interaction development:
  A. The Exile — Jaromír's story (where he went, what he wants, what
     Vratislav owes or fears) is the central character-level tension of
     this opening.
  B. The Bishop-Friend — Jan of Opava as confidant and Church liaison;
     a duke's relationship with a loyal bishop is one of the oldest
     narrative forms in the medieval repertoire.
  C. The Polish Wife — Šwiétosława's perspective: a foreign noblewoman
     in a Czech court navigating family politics, faith, and a husband
     whose rival is an exiled Přemyslid kinsman.
  D. The Knight — Záviš (prowess 10, courtier) as the archetypal sworn
     sword and household companion; his relationships with the other
     knights, the children, and the court shape the texture of daily life.
  E. The Succession Question — which son inherits? The hooked three
     (Boleslav, Bořivoj, Vladislav) versus the un-hooked Břetislav; the
     daughters' marriage trajectories; a family with six children and one
     title is inherently a story about who gets what and at what cost.

Bottom line:
  Playtest 2 opens in a materially similar position to Playtest 1 but with
  a character web that is immediately more narratively interesting: the rival
  is in exile rather than entrenched, the theocracy seat is held by a friend
  rather than an enemy, and the Pope is already displeased. Mechanically
  this is a slightly easier position than Playtest 1 (no un-revokable rival
  vassal in the realm); narratively it is richer, because the danger is
  mobile and the stakes are personal. The Jaromír question is the thread
  to pull first.
